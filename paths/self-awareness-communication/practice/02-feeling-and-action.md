# 2. Name a feeling without making it an instruction

## Aim

A feeling is information about your experience; it does not determine the next action.

Use ordinary words such as frustrated, disappointed or uncertain if they fit. You do not need a perfect label to choose a respectful response. Separate the feeling from a judgement about the other person and from the action you might take next.

## Mechanism and practical trade-off

Some words that sound like feelings contain interpretations: ignored can imply someone intended exclusion. Translate them into an emotion and the specific event when possible. This does not invalidate the experience. It makes the concern clearer and gives the other person a chance to address the event rather than defend against a presumed motive.

## Fictional case

Dana feels frustrated when a shared document changes shortly before a meeting.

## Your practice

Write a feeling, concern and useful next action.

Observation / known facts: ________________________________________

Interpretation / uncertainty: _____________________________________

Proposed next action and timing: __________________________________

Constraint or exception: __________________________________________

Evidence to review afterwards: ____________________________________

## Worked fictional response

I feel frustrated. I am concerned about preparing from the correct version. I will ask which sections changed and request a stable copy before the meeting. This describes Dana’s experience without assuming deliberate sabotage.

## Self-check

- The feeling differs from the proposed action.
- The concern names an actual practical effect.
- Compare your answer with the example. Explain any different choice.
