# 1. Separate what happened from what it means

## Aim

An observation describes what could be seen or heard; an interpretation explains it and may be wrong.

Both are useful, but keeping them separate creates room to ask questions. Avoid treating a guess about someone’s motives as a fact. A precise observation can be checked together without requiring the other person to accept your whole explanation.

## Mechanism and practical trade-off

Interpretations become especially risky when they attribute intent: deliberate avoidance cannot be observed from silence alone. Ask for information relevant to the task rather than demanding an explanation of character. If repeated missed replies cause a practical problem, that pattern can still be discussed using dates and effects without claiming certainty about motives.

## Fictional case

Ravi sees a teammate leave a message unanswered for two hours and concludes she does not respect him.

## Your practice

Split the event into observation and interpretation, then write a neutral question.

Observation / known facts: ________________________________________

Interpretation / uncertainty: _____________________________________

Proposed next action and timing: __________________________________

Constraint or exception: __________________________________________

Evidence to review afterwards: ____________________________________

## Worked fictional response

Observation: my message has no reply after two hours. Interpretation: she does not respect me. Question: when would be a good time to confirm the plan? She may be busy, offline or waiting for information.

## Self-check

- Observation and motive interpretation are separated.
- The question seeks missing information neutrally.
- The worked response is used for comparison, with any justified alternative explained.
